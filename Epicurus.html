Epicurus (c. 341-270 BC)

Epicurus (341 - 270 BCE) was an ancient Greek philosopher who founded Epicureanism, a school of philosophy focused on achieving happiness and living a peaceful life. He believed that true happiness did not come from wealth, power, or luxury, but from reducing unnecessary desires, overcoming fears, building meaningful friendships, and living wisely. For Epicurus, the goal of life was to experience pleasure in a thoughtful and balanced way, while avoiding unnecessary pain and anxiety.

Epicurus established a philosophical school in Athens known as the Garden, where he and his followers studied philosophy and shared their ideas. The school encouraged a simple lifestyle, with modest meals and an emphasis on friendship and self-sufficiency. Epicurus believed that people could achieve a happier life by appreciating what they already had rather than constantly pursuing more. He also argued that fear of death and punishment from the gods prevented people from living peacefully..

His views on religion were unusual for his time. Epicurus believed that the gods existed but did not interfere in human affairs or punish people for their actions. He also believed that the natural world could be explained through physical processes rather than divine intervention. Following the earlier philosopher Democritus, he argued that everything was made of tiny particles called atoms, which moved through empty space and combined to form different things.

Epicurus also introduced the idea that atoms could occasionally make a slight, unpredictable change in their movement, known as the atomic swerve. This idea differed from Democritus' theory and helped Epicurus explain how human choices might be possible in a universe governed by physical processes. His philosophy therefore combined ideas about nature with questions about freedom, happiness, and human behaviour.

Epicurus reportedly wrote more than 300 works covering a wide range of philosophical subjects, but most have been lost. Some of his letters and a collection of his main teachings have survived, while much of what is known about his ideas comes from later writers, including the Roman poet Lucretius. Despite the loss of many original texts, his philosophy continued to influence thinkers long after his death.

During the Middle Ages, Epicurus was often misunderstood and incorrectly associated with excessive indulgence and a life of luxury. His actual teachings emphasised moderation, peace of mind, and the enjoyment of simple pleasures. His ideas became more widely studied again from the Renaissance onward and influenced later philosophers, particularly during the Enlightenment. Epicureanism remains significant today for its focus on well-being, friendship, and the importance of living without unnecessary fear.
